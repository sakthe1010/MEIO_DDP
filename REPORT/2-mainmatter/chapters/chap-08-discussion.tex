\section{Answering the Research Questions}
\label{sec:disc:rq}

Five research questions (RQs) motivated this DDP.  This section
evaluates each against the experimental evidence from
Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\subsection{RQ1: Does joint inventory--transport optimisation outperform
single-axis optimisation?}

\textbf{Answer: Yes, decisively.}

Experiment E3 (joint optimisation) achieves a total cost of \$261,248
at a fill rate of 98.72\%.  This is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{37.3\% below the analytical baseline (E0)}, which costs
        \$416,952 at only 91.59\% fill.
  \item \textbf{29.5\% below transport-only optimisation (E1b)}, which
        costs \$370,370 but achieves only 87.44\% fill.
  \item \textbf{44.8\% below inventory-only optimisation (E2)}, which
        costs \$473,470 even while achieving 97.73\% fill.
\end{itemize}

The mechanism is clear from the cost-shift analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:results:e3}: joint optimisation deliberately increases
holding cost by \$60k ($+$288\%) to unlock \$194k in transport savings
($-$58\%) and a further \$22k in shortage avoidance ($-$86\%), a
roughly 3.5:1 leverage between inventory cost spent and total cost
returned.

The reason single-axis optimisation cannot achieve this is structural.
Transport-only optimisation (E1b) cannot raise dispatch thresholds
without depleting retailer inventory, because base-stock levels are
anchored at lean analytical values.  Inventory-only optimisation (E2)
raises stock levels but leaves dispatch thresholds at zero, so every
order is dispatched immediately as a small, expensive partial load.
Only joint search discovers that raising stock and raising dispatch
thresholds are \emph{complementary} moves that must be made
simultaneously.

Furthermore, the per-SKU variant (E3\_per\_sku,
Section~\ref{sec:results:e3_per_sku}) shows that the same approach
can deliver an even stricter service guarantee — every SKU above 98.5\%
fill — at a cost premium of only \$6.5k (2.5\%) over the
aggregate-constraint E3.

\subsection{RQ2: How does the joint-optimised configuration perform
under supply disruption?}
\label{sec:disc:rq2}

\textbf{Answer: It absorbs the shock substantially better than the
analytical baseline, even though the optimiser never saw the
disruption.}

Experiment E5 (Section~\ref{sec:results:e5}) injects a 14-day outage at
warehouse W1 in the middle of the steady-state measurement window and
applies the same shock to E0 (analytical) parameters and E3
(joint-optimised) parameters.  The result is striking:

\begin{itemize}
  \item E3 parameters: \$2,222,406 total cost, 81.86\% fill rate
        despite the outage.
  \item E0 parameters: \$2,577,516 total cost, 73.83\% fill rate.
  \item E3 outperforms E0 by 8.0 percentage points of fill rate and
        13.8\% of total cost.
\end{itemize}

The mechanism is incidental robustness.  E3 holds higher safety stock
at the warehouses (holding cost shifts from 5.0\% to 31.1\% of total
in steady state) for transport-consolidation reasons.  When W1 cannot
ship for two weeks, this elevated stock continues to serve R1 and R2
longer than the lean E0 buffer can.  The optimisation criterion was
``minimise cost subject to a fill-rate constraint'' — disruption
absorption was an unintended bonus.

This is encouraging from a deployment standpoint: the configurations
recommended by the framework are not brittle to common operational
shocks, even though the framework currently does not optimise for them
explicitly.  Disruption-aware joint optimisation (where the optimiser
sees the disruption while searching) is a natural future extension.

\subsection{RQ3: Does joint optimisation suppress the bullwhip effect?}

\textbf{Answer: Steady-state bullwhip is essentially $1.0$ in this
scenario, so there is nothing to suppress.}

The corrected per-echelon bullwhip metric
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:bullwhip}) reports ratios in the range
$0.998$--$1.004$ at every (node, SKU) pair across E0, E3, and
E3\_per\_sku.  Under base-stock policies and deterministic lead times,
each node's outgoing orders equal its incoming demand; there is no
amplification to suppress.  This rules out a simpler narrative — ``E3
suppresses bullwhip'' — that the original (incorrectly aggregated)
metric appeared to support.

The bullwhip metric is not vacuous, however; it is informative under
two conditions:

\begin{description}
  \item[Policy choice (E6).] $(s,S)$ produces per-(node, SKU) bullwhip
        ratios up to 176 due to discrete batching;
        periodic\_review produces ratios up to 13.9 due to review-cycle
        delay.  These policies generate bullwhip endogenously.
  \item[Supply shock (E5).] During the W1 outage, retailer ratios spike
        to 30--80 — the metric correctly registers the disturbance.
\end{description}

The thesis claim therefore becomes: \emph{joint optimisation does not
introduce bullwhip; choice of policy does.}  This also explains the
negative result of E8: the bullwhip-aware optimiser is most useful when
the policy or scenario already induces bullwhip, which the canonical
base-stock + deterministic-LT setup does not.

\subsection{RQ4: Does the echelon base-stock policy dominate empirically?}

\textbf{Answer: No, not under transport-cost-dominated conditions with
deterministic lead times.}

Experiment E6 (Section~\ref{sec:results:e6}) optimises four policies on
the same scenario and finds that:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $(s,S)$ is the cheapest feasible policy at \$352,015 / 94.33\%
        fill — its discrete batching aligns naturally with vehicle
        loads.
  \item base\_stock is second at \$458,021 / 96.29\%.
  \item periodic\_review is \emph{infeasible} at \$409,051 / 88.48\% —
        the review-cycle delay collides with the transport layer's
        consolidation logic to produce stockouts.
  \item echelon\_stock is the most expensive at \$486,394 / 98.46\% —
        it inflates holding cost without finding a corresponding
        transport saving in this regime.
\end{itemize}

The textbook expectation that echelon base-stock dominates rests on the
Clark--Scarf assumptions (serial network, no capacity constraints,
independent demands) which the canonical 1N3 scenario does not satisfy.
In particular, the divergent topology (W1 serves \{R1, R2\}; W2 serves
R3) breaks the serial assumption, and the vehicle-capacity transport
layer breaks the no-capacity assumption.  The empirical winner is
$(s,S)$ for the inventory layer alone, but neither $(s,S)$ nor
echelon\_stock beats the joint base-stock + dispatch-threshold optimum
of E3 (\$261k).  The joint optimisation across both layers is more
valuable than the choice of inventory policy within either.

\subsection{RQ5: How robust is the joint optimum to demand-forecast error?}

\textbf{Answer: Robust up to $\sigma_f \approx 10\%$; degrades gracefully
to $\sigma_f \approx 20\%$; needs re-fitting beyond.}

Experiment E7 (Section~\ref{sec:results:e7}) injects multiplicative
forecast error and shows a clean monotone degradation, summarised
in Table~\ref{tab:e7}: cost rises from \$262k at $\sigma_f = 0\%$
to \$337k at $\sigma_f = 30\%$, while fill rate falls from 98.7\%
to 86.5\%.

Practical implication: a forecasting layer with relative error below
$\sim$10\% (well within reach of standard exponential smoothing or
GBM-based forecasters on M5-style data) can adopt the recommendations
in Section~\ref{sec:results:e3} directly.  Above 20\% error, the
configuration becomes infeasible against the 92\% target and the
optimisation should be re-run with the noisy oracle in the loop, which
the framework supports.

\section{Practical Recommendations}
\label{sec:disc:recommendations}

The experimental results translate into five actionable guidelines for
supply-chain practitioners.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Co-optimise inventory and dispatch when transport cost
        exceeds 50\% of total cost.}  In the E0 baseline transport
        accounts for 79.5\% of total cost — a strong signal that
        transport is the primary lever.  As a rule of thumb, if
        transport exceeds half the total cost, joint optimisation is
        likely to return at least 25\% savings.

  \item \textbf{Accept a deliberate holding-cost increase to unlock
        consolidation.}  The 3.5:1 leverage ratio observed in this
        study suggests that every additional dollar of safety stock
        invested in the warehouse layer returns approximately \$3.50
        in transport-plus-shortage savings.  This counter-intuitive
        trade-off is invisible to managers who optimise cost
        components independently.

  \item \textbf{Use the per-SKU constraint when service is contracted
        per-item.}  The canonical scenario shows that the per-SKU
        joint optimum (E3\_per\_sku) costs only 2.5\% more than the
        aggregate variant while guaranteeing every SKU above 98.5\%
        fill — a small premium for a materially stronger service
        guarantee.

  \item \textbf{Use the NSGA-II Pareto frontier to negotiate
        service-level agreements.}  The smooth two-objective frontier
        in E4 directly prices each percentage point of fill rate:
        marginal cost is roughly \$8k/pp below 97\% and \$15--20k/pp
        between 98\% and 99.8\%.  Mapping this curve allows the
        supplier to price each incremental service percentage point
        accurately.

  \item \textbf{Forecast accuracy below 10\% relative error is
        sufficient.}  The joint optima are stable in this regime
        (E7).  Above 20\% forecast error the policy parameters should
        be refit using the noisy-oracle methodology of E7.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Scalability}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}

The following limitations of the current framework should be considered
when interpreting the results.

\begin{description}
  \item[Deterministic lead times.] The simulator uses a fixed,
        deterministic transit time on each lane.  Real transport
        networks exhibit stochastic lead times driven by congestion,
        weather, and carrier variability.  Extending to stochastic
        lead times would increase the optimal safety-stock levels and
        is one regime in which the echelon base-stock policy might
        regain the empirical advantage that E6 denied it.

  \item[Two-DC layer only.] The 1N3 topology has $N=2$ warehouses (W1,
        W2) serving the three retailers in a fixed assignment.  More
        complex networks (multi-tier hub-and-spoke, multiple
        suppliers, lateral assignment) may exhibit different
        consolidation dynamics.

  \item[CSV demand without an integrated forecaster.] Demand is read
        from historical M5 CSVs and the noisy oracle of E7 wraps that
        ground-truth series.  In production, policies are triggered
        by online forecasts, not exact future demand; integrating a
        live forecaster (e.g.\ exponential smoothing or LightGBM)
        is the natural next step.

  \item[No lateral transshipment.] Retailers cannot transfer stock to
        each other.  Lateral transshipment is a common strategy for
        balancing inventory across retail locations and could
        substantially reduce shortage costs, particularly under
        disruption.

  \item[Steady-state bullwhip is uninformative.] Under base-stock
        policies and deterministic lead times the bullwhip metric is
        $\approx 1$ everywhere, so the bullwhip-aware optimiser of E8
        has nothing to act on.  Stress regimes (E5 disruption, E6
        $(s,S)$ policy) reveal non-trivial bullwhip; reporting
        bullwhip in the canonical scenario therefore measures absence
        of amplification rather than its degree.

  \item[Disruption-aware optimisation not yet implemented.] E5
        characterises the \emph{incidental} robustness of the
        cost-only optimum.  An optimiser that sees the disruption
        scenario at search time would in principle find a more robust
        configuration, possibly at a small steady-state cost premium.
        This is reserved for future work.

  \item[Demand sampling variance not quantified.] All main experiments
        (E0--E8) are conditioned on the single realised M5 Walmart
        demand trace; no confidence intervals around cost or fill-rate
        estimates are reported.  Experiment E9 establishes that the
        E3 parameters transfer to Poisson demand (same mean, independent
        realisations), but a bootstrapped or model-based demand
        generator would be needed to produce valid credible intervals.
\end{description}

\subsection{Computational scalability}
\label{sec:disc:scalability}

The 1N3 ($N=2$) topology with five SKUs produces a 30-dimensional
joint search space: 25 integer base-stock levels (five SKUs for each
of five non-supplier nodes) and five continuous dispatch thresholds
(one per lane).  Table~\ref{tab:opt-dims} (Chapter~\ref{chap:optimization})
shows that the $(s,S)$ and periodic-review E6 variants reach 35
dimensions.  At this scale, TPE-based Bayesian optimisation converges
reliably within 100--200 trials, as confirmed by the E3 convergence
trace (Section~\ref{sec:results:e3}).

Scaling to larger networks increases the joint search space rapidly.
A 10-node, 10-SKU network with 15 lanes would produce
approximately $9 \times 10 = 90$ inventory parameters plus
15 dispatch thresholds, giving a 105-dimensional joint space.
\textsc{Optuna}'s TPE implementation is commonly cited as tractable
up to $\sim\!50$ dimensions \citep{AkibaEtAl2019}; beyond that
threshold sample efficiency degrades and wall-clock time grows
substantially.  Three mitigations would be appropriate at scale:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hierarchical decomposition.}  Optimise each echelon
        layer independently, then coordinate at the boundary.
        This reduces a 105-dimensional joint problem to a sequence of
        smaller sub-problems at the cost of losing cross-echelon
        synergies.
  \item \textbf{Warm-starting from analytical bounds.}  Newsvendor
        levels provide a good starting point; confining the search to
        $[\mu_{\text{NV}},\, 2 \mu_{\text{NV}}]$ rather than a broad
        multiplier range tightens the effective search space.
  \item \textbf{Per-SKU-family decomposition.}  If demand across SKUs
        is weakly correlated, each SKU family can be optimised
        independently, reducing dimensionality by a factor equal to
        the number of families.
\end{enumerate}
These mitigations do not affect the validity of the results reported
for the canonical 1N3, 5-SKU scenario.  They are noted here to
characterise the practical scope of the framework without modification.
