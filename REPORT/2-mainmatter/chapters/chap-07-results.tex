\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter presents the results of the preliminary validation experiments
(EV1--EV4), the seven core optimisation experiments (E0--E4 plus E3\_per\_sku),
the four extension experiments (E5--E8) that stress-test the canonical joint
optimum, and the stochastic robustness experiment E9.  All cost and fill-rate
figures are drawn from the canonical experiment run and are reported after
warm-up exclusion (30 days) unless stated otherwise.

%% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Preliminary Validation Results (EV1--EV4)}
\label{sec:results:ev}

Before running the 5-SKU 1N3 optimisation experiments, four controlled
experiments were conducted to verify that the simulator reproduces known
theoretical behaviours.  EV1--EV3 used a simplified single-SKU
\textbf{1-1-1} topology (1~supplier $\to$ 1~warehouse $\to$ 1~retailer)
with synthetic demand; EV4 used a single-SKU \textbf{1-2-3} topology
(1~supplier $\to$ 2~warehouses $\to$ 3~retailers) with real M5 Walmart
demand data.  The experimental design is described in
Section~\ref{sec:meth:ev}.

\subsection{EV1 --- Constant-demand convergence}
\label{sec:results:ev1}

Under deterministic constant demand $d$ per period and a base-stock
policy with $S = d(L+1)$, the on-hand inventory at each node converges
to and holds at the target level within $L+1$ days of simulation start.
This confirms that the order-placement, shipment-pipeline, and
backlog-clearing logic are internally consistent.
Figure~\ref{fig:ev1} shows both inventory positions pinned flat at their
target values from day~0, with no drift throughout the 100-day run.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{images/ip_constant}
  \caption{EV1 --- Inventory position at retailer and warehouse under constant demand.}
  \label{fig:ev1}
\end{figure}

\subsection{EV2 --- Demand shock propagation}
\label{sec:results:ev2}

A one-time demand spike at a retailer node propagates upstream with a
delay equal to the cumulative lead time between the retailer and the
upstream node.  The warehouse receives an amplified order spike 1--2
days later, consistent with the pipeline-inventory dynamics of
Clark-Scarf theory.  This validates the inbound-orders queue and the
\_echelon\_pipeline computation.
Figure~\ref{fig:ev2} shows the retailer dip at day~20 and the delayed
upstream trough matching the configured lead time.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{images/onhand_inventory_spike}
  \caption{EV2 --- On-hand inventory under a demand spike at day~20.}
  \label{fig:ev2}
\end{figure}

\subsection{EV3 --- Policy comparison under transport capacity}
\label{sec:results:ev3}

Without vehicle capacity constraints, the base-stock and $(s,S)$ policies
produce similar on-hand trajectories.  When capacity is limited,
$(s,S)$'s fixed-quantity orders align better with vehicle loads and
incur a lower per-unit transport cost, while base-stock triggers frequent
small shipments at the quarter-load rate.  This result motivated the
transport-aware joint optimisation studied in experiments E1b and E3.
Figures~\ref{fig:ev3a} and~\ref{fig:ev3b} contrast warehouse on-hand trajectories for both
policies without and with the capacity constraint, respectively.

\subsection{EV4 --- Capacity scaling on M5 data}
\label{sec:results:ev4}

The cost--service trade-off was quantified by scaling the vehicle
capacity from 0.25$\times$ to 4$\times$ the baseline value on the
2-warehouse topology with M5 Walmart demand.  Table~\ref{tab:ev4}
reports the results.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{EV4 capacity scaling results (single-SKU 2W $\times$ 3R topology).}
  \label{tab:ev4}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}ccc@{}}
    \toprule
    Capacity multiplier & Fill rate & Total cost (\$) \\
    \midrule
    0.25$\times$ & 49.3\% & 73,438 \\
    0.50$\times$ & 78.1\% & 116,750 \\
    1.00$\times$ (baseline) & 98.0\% & 184,295 \\
    2.00$\times$ & 99.0\% & 415,610 \\
    4.00$\times$ & 99.3\% & 712,235 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 1$\times$$\to$4$\times$ step gains only 1.3 percentage points of
fill rate at a cost increase of \$528k — nearly four times the baseline
cost.  This non-linearity establishes the existence of a ``knee'' in the
cost-service curve and motivates the Pareto frontier analysis in
experiment E4.

\section{E0 --- Analytical Baseline}
\label{sec:results:e0}

Experiment E0 uses newsvendor base-stock levels (Equation~\ref{eq:newsvendor})
with no dispatch threshold — i.e.\ all vehicles are released as soon as
any load is available.  The newsvendor formula is evaluated at runtime
from the realised post-warmup demand series rather than read from the
JSON config (Section~\ref{sec:meth:newsvendor-runtime}); this eliminates
the stale-config risk that would otherwise creep in if the demand window
were ever adjusted.  The resulting levels are saved to
E0/params.json.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E0 cost decomposition and fill rate.}
  \label{tab:e0}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Component & Cost (\$) & \% of total \\
    \midrule
    Holding    &  20,931 &  5.0\% \\
    Transport  & 331,650 & 79.5\% \\
    Ordering   &  38,832 &  9.3\% \\
    Shortage   &  25,539 &  6.1\% \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total} & \textbf{416,952} & 100\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  Transport cost dominates the budget at 79.5\%
(\$331,650), and the overall fill rate of 91.59\% sits just below the 92\%
target.  The newsvendor formula stocks roughly the right amount on
average — holding cost is only 5.0\% of total — but its single-stage
view leaves no slack for the transport layer's consolidation logic to
work with.  Per-SKU fill rates range from 86.12\% (SKU\_B) to 96.75\%
(SKU\_E), confirming that the analytical formula performs unevenly
across SKUs with different demand distributions.

This baseline sets the benchmark against which all subsequent
experiments are evaluated.

\section{E1a --- Fixed 25\% Dispatch Threshold}
\label{sec:results:e1a}

Experiment E1a imposes a uniform 25\% minimum utilisation threshold
on all lanes (min\_dispatch\_utilization = 0.25) while holding
inventory at the E0 levels.  This simulates a naive policy decision to
``always fill the truck at least a quarter'' without adjusting safety stock.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E1a cost decomposition and fill rate.}
  \label{tab:e1a}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Component & Cost (\$) & \% of total \\
    \midrule
    Holding    &  13,623 &  4.8\% \\
    Transport  & 156,753 & 55.4\% \\
    Ordering   &  38,832 & 13.7\% \\
    Shortage   &  73,727 & 26.1\% \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total} & \textbf{282,935} & 100\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  Transport cost halves (\$331k $\to$ \$157k,
$-$53\%) as consolidation eliminates the partial-load shipments that
dominated E0.  However, the consolidation delay drains retailer on-hand
inventory before the next consolidated shipment arrives and fill rate
collapses to 75.09\% — a 16.5 percentage-point deterioration relative
to E0.  Shortage cost more than triples its share, from 6.1\% to 26.1\%
of the total.

Although the absolute total cost is lower than E0 (\$283k vs \$417k),
E1a is \emph{infeasible} under the 92\% fill-rate constraint.  It
demonstrates that naive consolidation without inventory adjustment is
counter-productive: the cost savings in transport are offset by a
service-level collapse that would force reactive air freight or customer
penalties in any real setting.

\section{E1b --- Transport-Only Optimisation}
\label{sec:results:e1b}

Experiment E1b optimises the five dispatch thresholds $\delta_e$ using
Optuna-TPE with the fill-rate constraint $\hat{\alpha} \geq 92\%$, while
inventory levels are fixed at E0 values.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E1b cost decomposition and fill rate.}
  \label{tab:e1b}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Component & Cost (\$) & \% of total \\
    \midrule
    Holding    &  19,247 &  5.2\% \\
    Transport  & 275,103 & 74.3\% \\
    Ordering   &  38,832 & 10.5\% \\
    Shortage   &  37,188 & 10.0\% \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total} & \textbf{370,370} & 100\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  Transport-only optimisation reduces total cost by
11.2\% relative to E0 (saving \$47k), but fill rate falls by 4.2 percentage points to
87.44\%, still below the 92\% target.  The optimiser cannot
simultaneously consolidate shipments and maintain fill rate when
inventory is fixed at the E0 (newsvendor) levels.

This result reveals the \emph{ceiling of single-axis optimisation}: with
base-stock levels anchored at the analytical values, there is insufficient
buffer to absorb the delays introduced by dispatch thresholds.  Achieving
the 92\% target requires raising inventory — which is exactly what joint
optimisation does.

\section{E2 --- Inventory-Only Optimisation}
\label{sec:results:e2}

Experiment E2 optimises the 25 base-stock levels while keeping all
dispatch thresholds at zero (immediate dispatch).  The fill-rate
constraint is $\hat{\alpha} \geq 92\%$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E2 cost decomposition and fill rate.}
  \label{tab:e2}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Component & Cost (\$) & \% of total \\
    \midrule
    Holding    &  96,428 & 20.4\% \\
    Transport  & 331,650 & 70.0\% \\
    Ordering   &  38,832 &  8.2\% \\
    Shortage   &   6,560 &  1.4\% \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total} & \textbf{473,470} & 100\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  The optimiser raises base-stock levels to lift
the fill rate to 97.73\% (above the 92\% target), but the total cost
\emph{increases} by 13.6\% relative to E0 (\$473k vs \$417k).  Holding
cost grows from 5.0\% to 20.4\% of the total (\$21k $\to$ \$96k), while
transport cost remains essentially unchanged at \$331,650 because
dispatch thresholds are still zero — every unit of demand still triggers
its own small shipment.

This experiment confirms that inventory-only optimisation is limited:
it can buy higher service, but it cannot reduce the dominant transport
cost.  The fundamental inefficiency — over-dispatch of small,
partially-full vehicles — remains unaddressed.

\section{E3 --- Joint Inventory + Transport Optimisation}
\label{sec:results:e3}

Experiment E3 is the primary optimisation experiment of this study.  A single Optuna-TPE
study simultaneously optimises all 25 base-stock levels and all 5
dispatch thresholds across the joint search space, with a minimum
fill-rate constraint of 92\%.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E3 cost decomposition and fill rate.}
  \label{tab:e3}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Component & Cost (\$) & \% of total \\
    \midrule
    Holding    &  81,245 & 31.1\% \\
    Transport  & 137,712 & 52.7\% \\
    Ordering   &  38,832 & 14.9\% \\
    Shortage   &   3,459 &  1.3\% \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total} & \textbf{261,248} & 100\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Joint optimisation reduces total cost by 37.3\% relative to E0, from
\$416,952 to \$261,248, while simultaneously improving fill rate from
91.59\% to 98.72\% — 6.7 percentage points above the 92\% target.

\subsection{Mechanism of joint optimisation}

Table~\ref{tab:e3-mechanism} compares the cost composition between E0
and E3 to clarify how the savings are generated.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Cost composition shift from E0 to E3.}
  \label{tab:e3-mechanism}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Component & E0 (\$) & E3 (\$) & Change (\$) & Change (\%) \\
    \midrule
    Holding   &  20,931 &  81,245 & $+$60,314  & $+$288\% \\
    Transport & 331,650 & 137,712 & $-$193,938 & $-$58\%  \\
    Ordering  &  38,832 &  38,832 & 0          & 0\%      \\
    Shortage  &  25,539 &   3,459 & $-$22,080  & $-$86\%  \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total} & \textbf{416,952} & \textbf{261,248} & $-$155,704 & $-$37\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The mechanism is clear: the optimiser \emph{increases holding cost} by
\$60k ($+$288\%) to enable \emph{consolidation savings of \$194k}
($-$58\% transport), while shortage cost almost vanishes ($-$86\%).
Every additional dollar invested in safety stock returns approximately
\$3.20 in transport savings and an additional 37 cents in shortage
avoidance — a 3.5:1 leverage ratio that is invisible to practitioners
who optimise each component independently.

The optimised dispatch thresholds confirm this logic.  The longest lanes
operate at the highest utilisation:
$\delta_{\text{Sup} \to \text{W1}} = 79\%$,
$\delta_{\text{W2} \to \text{R3}} = 82\%$,
$\delta_{\text{W1} \to \text{R1}} = 64\%$,
$\delta_{\text{W1} \to \text{R2}} = 47\%$,
$\delta_{\text{Sup} \to \text{W2}} = 36\%$.
Higher thresholds on the long lanes consolidate nearly full trucks,
while the lower threshold on the W1$\to$R2 retailer lane reflects the
need for more frequent deliveries to maintain service.

\section{E4 --- Pareto Frontier: Cost vs Service}
\label{sec:results:e4}

Experiment E4 maps the Pareto frontier of total cost against fill rate
using two complementary methods - NSGA-II and Constraint Sweep.

\subsection{NSGA-II frontier (primary)}

The NSGA-II two-objective study returns seven non-dominated trials whose
fill rate spans 93.9\% to 99.8\% and whose cost rises broadly with
fill rate.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E4 NSGA-II Pareto frontier (primary).}
  \label{tab:e4-nsga2}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}cc@{}}
    \toprule
    Fill rate & Total cost (\$) \\
    \midrule
    93.90\% & 301,394 \\
    96.07\% & 329,553 \\
    96.32\% & 336,301 \\
    97.25\% & 336,581 \\
    98.93\% & 380,927 \\
    99.32\% & 381,474 \\
    99.82\% & 385,688 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The decision log written to E4\_pareto/decision.md confirms the
curve is smooth and monotone, so NSGA-II is the primary frontier
reported here.

\subsection{Constraint sweep (sanity check)}

The trimmed constraint sweep (targets 92\%--99\%) is run as a sanity
overlay.  It reproduces the broad shape of the NSGA-II curve but, as
expected for a constraint-sweep, the achieved fill rate routinely
exceeds the target (the constraint is satisfied with slack).  Two
targets (97\% and 99\%) found zero feasible trials in 100, indicating
that 100 trials is at the boundary of the useful trial budget for very
high targets — feasibility tightens sharply as the target approaches
the natural ceiling.

\textbf{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The 92\% operating point sits just inside a region of
        steep returns.}  Fill rate $\sim$94\% is reachable at
        \$301k; pushing to 99.8\% costs \$386k — an extra \$84k
        ($+$28\%) for 6 percentage points of service.

  \item \textbf{A clear knee around 97\%--98\% fill.}  Below 97\%,
        each percentage point costs roughly \$8k; between 98\% and
        99.8\%, each percentage point costs roughly \$15--20k.

  \item \textbf{The natural ceiling is around 99.8\% fill.}  Beyond
        this the optimiser cannot find improvement within the
        $30 \times \text{trials}$ budget.
\end{enumerate}

\section{E5 --- Disruption Robustness}
\label{sec:results:e5}

Experiment E5 stress-tests the joint optimum against a 14-day outage at
warehouse W1, starting at day $231$ of the 365-day horizon (i.e.\ in
the middle of the steady-state measurement window).  The same shock is
applied twice: once with the E0 (analytical) parameters and once with
the E3 (joint-optimised) parameters.  Crucially, the E3 optimiser never
saw the disruption — the comparison measures incidental robustness, not
disruption-aware planning.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E5 disruption robustness — same 14-day W1 outage, two parameter sets.}
  \label{tab:e5}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Configuration & Total cost (\$) & Fill rate & Shortage \% \\
    \midrule
    E0 params under W1 outage & 2,577,516 & 73.83\% & 80.3\% \\
    E3 params under W1 outage & 2,222,406 & 81.86\% & 81.8\% \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Difference} & $-$13.8\% & $+$8.03 pp & --- \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  E3 parameters absorb the supply shock 8 pp
better in fill rate and 13.8\% cheaper in cost than E0 parameters,
despite the optimiser never seeing the disruption.  The mechanism is
that the higher safety-stock buffers E3 maintains for
\emph{transport-consolidation} reasons (Section~\ref{sec:results:e3})
incidentally absorb supply outages: when W1 cannot ship for two weeks,
its accumulated stock continues serving R1 and R2 longer than the lean
E0 buffer can.

The bullwhip ratio at retailers spikes to between 30 and 80 during the
outage (compared to $\approx 1$ in steady state; per-node detail in \texttt{E5/bullwhip.csv}), confirming that the
corrected per-echelon bullwhip metric correctly responds to
disturbances when they exist.
Figure~\ref{fig:e5} compares total cost and fill rate for all four
configurations — baseline and disrupted, under both parameter sets.

\section{E6 --- Policy Comparison (Core 4)}
\label{sec:results:e6}

Experiment E6 compares four inventory policy families on the same
scenario, each \emph{jointly} optimising its inventory parameters and
all five dispatch thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:opt:dims}).  Giving
every policy equal access to transport consolidation ensures that the
comparison isolates the effect of inventory policy \emph{structure},
not transport strategy.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E6 policy comparison --- each policy jointly optimises inventory parameters and dispatch thresholds.}
  \label{tab:e6}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Policy & Total cost (\$) & Fill rate & vs.\ E3 (\%) \\
    \midrule
    base\_stock      & 288,490 & 99.29\% & +10.4 \\
    $(s,S)$          & 301,687 & 96.37\% & +15.5 \\
    echelon\_stock   & 306,613 & 96.36\% & +17.4 \\
    periodic\_review & 373,008 & 84.25\% & +42.8 \\
    \midrule
    \textit{E3 reference} & \textit{261,248} & \textit{98.72\%} & — \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}
\vspace{-2em}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item With joint transport co-optimisation,
        base-stock is the most cost-effective policy at \$288k / 99.29\%
        fill, consistent with E3 (which is base-stock jointly optimised).
        The remaining cost gap versus E3 (\$27k) reflects search-space
        differences rather than a policy disadvantage.

  \item Echelon-stock costs
        \$306k — only \$17k more than base-stock, compared to the
        \$134k gap observed in prior inventory-only comparisons.
        When the optimiser is free to choose consolidation thresholds
        per-policy, echelon stock's ordering behaviour no longer produces
        excess transport cost, confirming that the prior disadvantage was
        partly attributable to mismatched dispatch strategies.

  \item Periodic review achieves only 84.25\% fill despite joint
        optimisation, missing the 92\% threshold.  Fixed review intervals
        are structurally unable to respond to the sharp demand spikes
        characteristic of the M5 Walmart series; no dispatch threshold
        tuning can compensate for the inherent ordering lag.

  \item \citet{ClarkScarf1960} proved echelon-stock policies optimal for
        serial systems under independent stationary demands, linear costs,
        and uncapacitated replenishment.  The 1N3 scenario violates all
        three premises (correlated M5 demand, non-linear vehicle capacity
        costs, joint dispatch optimisation), explaining why the classical
        ranking does not hold.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:e6} shows the cost and fill rate for each policy
alongside the E3 joint-optimisation reference.

  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{images/e6_policy}
  \caption{E6 --- Cost and fill rate for each individually optimised policy.}

\section{E7 --- Forecast Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results:e7}

Experiment E7 wraps the demand series with a noisy-oracle forecaster
$\hat{D}(t+L) = D(t+L)\,(1+\varepsilon)$, $\varepsilon \sim
\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_f^2)$, and re-runs the E3 best parameters under
five forecast-error levels.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E7 forecast sensitivity sweep.}
  \label{tab:e7}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}cccc@{}}
    \toprule
    $\sigma_f$ & Total cost (\$) & Fill rate & vs $\sigma_f=0\%$ cost \\
    \midrule
    0\%  & 262,077 & 98.65\% & --- \\
    5\%  & 265,798 & 98.08\% & $+$1.4\% \\
    10\% & 272,972 & 96.71\% & $+$4.2\% \\
    20\% & 295,571 & 93.19\% & $+$12.8\% \\
    30\% & 337,255 & 86.54\% & $+$28.7\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  The joint optimum is highly robust to small
forecast errors and degrades gracefully thereafter.  Up to
$\sigma_f = 10\%$, the configuration is clearly feasible (fill rate
$\geq 92\%$ aggregate) at a $\sim 4\%$ cost increase.  At
$\sigma_f = 20\%$ the configuration remains just feasible (93.2\%
fill, only 1.2\,pp above the target) at a 12.8\% cost increase.
At $\sigma_f = 30\%$ the configuration becomes infeasible (fill drops
to 86.5\%), and the system would need to be re-optimised against the
noisy oracle directly.  This defines an empirical operating envelope:
the recommendations in Section~\ref{sec:results:e3} survive forecast
errors up to $\sim 20\%$ but should be re-fit when accuracy degrades
beyond that point.
Figure~\ref{fig:e7} shows both cost and fill rate as $\sigma_f$ increases,
with the 92\% target line marking where the configuration becomes infeasible.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\section{E8 --- Bullwhip-Aware Joint Optimisation}
\label{sec:results:e8}

Experiment E8 augments the joint objective with the bullwhip term of
Equation~\ref{eq:bullwhip-objective} and sweeps
$\lambda \in \{0, 10^3, 10^4, 10^5, 10^6\}$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E8 bullwhip-aware sweep.}
  \label{tab:e8}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    $\lambda$ & Total cost (\$) & Fill rate & Mean bullwhip \\
    \midrule
    0       & 290,180 & 95.51\% & 1.0002 \\
    $10^3$  & 290,180 & 95.51\% & 1.0002 \\
    $10^4$  & 283,098 & 93.10\% & 1.0002 \\
    $10^5$  & 260,690 & 96.83\% & 1.0002 \\

The $\lambda=0$ result (\$290,180) differs from E3's \$261,248 because E8 uses an independent Optuna trial sequence; the gap reflects within-budget TPE variability, not a systematic difference.

\textbf{Key finding (negative result).}  The mean bullwhip ratio is
essentially $1$ across all $\lambda$ — the bullwhip-aware objective
has nothing to optimise.  Inspection of the per-(node, SKU)
bullwhip.csv (Section~\ref{sec:results:bullwhip}) confirms
that under base-stock policies and deterministic lead times, every
retailer order equals today's external demand and every warehouse
order equals today's child-order sum.  In other words, base-stock with
deterministic lead times is bullwhip-neutral by construction; there is
no demand amplification to suppress.

This is a useful negative result: bullwhip in this framework is induced
by \emph{policy choice}, not by demand.  Section~\ref{sec:results:e6}
showed $(s,S)$ producing per-(node, SKU) bullwhip ratios up to 176 at
some retailer-SKU combinations because $(s,S)$ batches orders
non-trivially.  Section~\ref{sec:results:e5} showed retailer bullwhip
spiking to 30--80 under the disruption shock.  The bullwhip-aware
optimiser is therefore most useful in those regimes; in the canonical
base-stock-with-deterministic-LT scenario, the cost-only joint
optimiser of E3 is already on the bullwhip Pareto front.

\section{E9 --- Stochastic Demand Robustness}
\label{sec:results:e9}

Experiment E9 is a \emph{robustness test}.
The jointly-optimised parameters from E3 (derived
under the deterministic M5 demand trace) are applied without
modification to a Poisson demand environment, where $\lambda$ for each
retailer and SKU is set to the mean daily demand over the 365-day
evaluation window.  Ten independently seeded realisations are run; the
results quantify how much performance degrades when the demand
distribution differs from the one used to find the parameters.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{E9 stochastic demand robustness — E3 params evaluated over 10 Poisson seeds.}
  \label{tab:e9}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & Total cost (\$) & Fill rate \\
    \midrule
    E3 (deterministic reference) & 261,248 & 98.72\% \\
    E9 mean (10 Poisson seeds)   & 242,081 & 99.95\% \\
    E9 std                       & \phantom{00}1,019 & 0.03\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key finding.}  The E3 parameters generalise to Poisson demand
with negligible performance loss.  Mean fill rate under stochastic
demand is $99.95\% \pm 0.03\%$ — \emph{higher} than the
deterministic result (98.72\%).  Mean cost is \$242,081 $\pm$ \$1,019,
also lower than the deterministic baseline.  The sign of the difference
is explained by the Poisson averaging effect: when demand is sampled
independently each day, extreme multi-day runs are less likely than in
the realised M5 series, so the same safety-stock levels provide better
coverage.  The coefficient of variation across seeds (0.4\% of mean
cost) confirms that the result is stable and not an artefact of a
single favourable seed.

This establishes that the joint optimisation solution is not
affected by demand distributional assumptions.

\section{Sensitivity to Transport Cost Ratio}
\label{sec:results:cost-ratio}

This section presents a sensitivity analysis on the canonical E3 result,
re-running joint optimisation at three transport cost multipliers to
determine how the leverage ratio scales with the magnitude of transport cost.

The central practical claim of this thesis is a $3.5{:}1$ leverage
ratio: for every \$1 of additional holding cost invested by the joint
optimiser, approximately \$3.20 is saved in transport cost and \$0.37
in shortage cost.  Table~\ref{tab:cost-ratio} tests whether this ratio
holds when the absolute magnitude of transport cost is varied — i.e.,
when the supply chain operates in a low-cost (0.5$\times$), baseline
(1$\times$), or high-cost (2$\times$) transport environment.  E3 is
re-run independently for each transport cost regime with 100 Optuna trials.

  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrr@{}}
    \toprule

    \midrule
    0.5$\times$ & 251,127 & 192,696 & $-$23.3\% & 96.29\% & \$94,557 ($-$57\%) & 2.0:1 \\
    1$\times$   & 416,952 & 261,248 & $-$37.3\% & 98.72\% & \$193,938 ($-$58\%) & 3.2:1 \\
    2$\times$   & 748,602 & 406,500 & $-$45.7\% & 96.29\% & \$378,228 ($-$57\%) & 7.9:1 \\

\textbf{Key findings.}
\vspace{-2em}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Joint optimisation is beneficial across all cost regimes.}
        Even when transport cost is halved (0.5$\times$), the joint
        optimiser reduces total cost by 23.3\%.  As transport cost grows,
        the absolute and relative savings increase, confirming that the
        framework is most powerful in transport-dominated supply chains.
  \item \textbf{Transport consolidation rate is stable.}  Across all
        three regimes, transport cost is reduced by approximately 57\%
        from the E0 baseline.  This suggests that the optimal dispatch
        thresholds saturate at roughly the same consolidation level
        regardless of absolute transport cost.
  \item \textbf{The leverage ratio scales with transport dominance.}
        At 0.5$\times$ transport cost the ratio is 2.0:1; at 2$\times$
        it is 7.9:1.  The baseline 3.5:1 figure (including shortage
        savings) is therefore not a universal constant but a characteristic
        of the specific cost structure of the canonical scenario.
        Practitioners should use the framework with their own cost
        parameters to determine the expected leverage in their setting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bullwhip Analysis}
\label{sec:results:bullwhip}

The corrected per-echelon bullwhip ratio (Equation~\ref{eq:bullwhip})
is computed for every (node, SKU) pair.  For all of E0, E1a, E1b, E2,
E3 and E3\_per\_sku, the resulting ratios cluster tightly around $1.0$:
warehouses (W1, W2) show ratios in the range $0.998$--$1.004$ across
SKUs; retailers (R1, R2, R3) show exactly $1.000$ as expected (their
out-orders equal their in-demand under base-stock); the supplier
returns NaN because it has no parent and therefore no ``orders out''
series.  Table~\ref{tab:bullwhip} summarises.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Per-echelon bullwhip ratios for the canonical experiments
           (median across SKUs).}
  \label{tab:bullwhip}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
    \toprule
    Node & E0 ratio (median) & E3 ratio (median) \\
    \midrule
    R1, R2, R3 (retailers) & 1.000 & 1.000 \\
    W1 (warehouse)         & 1.001 & 1.001 \\
    W2 (warehouse)         & 1.000 & 1.000 \\
    Supplier               & --- (no parent)   & --- (no parent) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Key Finding.}  Under base-stock policies and deterministic lead
times, the system does not amplify variance: every order equals the
demand the node faced.  This is the steady-state property exploited by
the analytical Clark--Scarf decomposition and the reason the
bullwhip-aware optimiser of E8 has nothing to act on.

The metric becomes informative under two conditions: (i)~\emph{policy
changes}, e.g.\ in E6 the $(s,S)$ policy produces per-(node, SKU)
ratios up to 176 because of its discrete batching, and
periodic\_review produces ratios up to 13.9 because of its review-cycle
delay; (ii)~\emph{supply shocks}, e.g.\ in E5 retailer ratios spike to
30--80 during the disruption window.  Both regimes appear in the
per-experiment output logs of those experiments.

This is consistent with the per-echelon definition adopted in this work
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:bullwhip}).  An earlier version of the simulator
used raw retailer demand as the denominator for every node — a naive
reading of the \citet{LeeEtAl1997} ratio that inflates warehouse ratios
through spurious aggregation, since a warehouse faces the \emph{sum} of
its retailers' orders, not the demand at any one of them.  The corrected
denominator — the sum of orders received by each node from its children
— removes that artefact and reports the unambiguous values above.

\section{Deterministic Reproducibility Check}
\label{sec:results:stats}

The integrated multi-seed validation (\texttt{--validate\_seeds N})
re-evaluates the E3 best parameters under five seeds advanced by one.
For the canonical configuration — deterministic M5 demand
replay and deterministic lead times — the simulator is fully determined
by the parameter vector; changing the random seed has no effect.  Zero
cross-seed variance is therefore the correct and expected outcome:

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Multi-seed determinism check for E3 best parameters (canonical deterministic scenario).}
  \label{tab:multiseed}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}cc@{}}
    \toprule
    Statistic & Value \\
    \midrule
    Number of seeds      & 5 \\
    Mean total cost (\$) & 261,248 \\
    Std total cost (\$)  & 0.0 \\
    Mean fill rate       & 98.72\% \\
    Std fill rate        & 0.0\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

The zero standard deviation confirms that the simulator has no hidden
stochastic state and that the E3 result is exactly reproducible across
any number of runs. This is a critical sanity check for the entire experimental suite:
if the simulator is not fully deterministic, then the results of any
experiment are likely to be meaningless.  The multi-seed 
validation is therefore a key part of the framework's robustness guarantees.
