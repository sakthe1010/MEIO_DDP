\chapter{Experimental Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter describes the experimental design, datasets, and evaluation
methodology used in this study.  The experiments are organised into three
groups: four preliminary validation experiments (EV1--EV4) on simplified
single-SKU topologies, seven core optimisation experiments
(E0--E4 plus E3\_per\_sku) on the full 5-SKU 1N3 network, and five
extension and robustness experiments (E5--E8 plus E9) that stress-test
the joint optimum under disruption, alternative inventory policies,
demand-forecast error, bullwhip-aware objectives, and stochastic demand.

%% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Preliminary Validation Experiments (EV1--EV4)}
\label{sec:meth:ev}

Before running the main optimisation suite, four controlled experiments
were designed to verify that the simulator correctly reproduces known
theoretical behaviour.  EV1--EV3 used a simplified \textbf{single-SKU, 1-1-1}
topology (1~supplier $\to$ 1~warehouse $\to$ 1~retailer) with synthetic demand
(constant 5~units/day for EV1 and EV3; a constant background with a single
spike at day~20 for EV2).  EV4 scaled to a
single-SKU \textbf{1-2-3} topology (1~supplier $\to$ 2~warehouses $\to$
3~retailers) and used real M5 Walmart demand data.

\subsection{EV1 --- Constant-demand convergence}
\label{sec:meth:ev1}

A constant daily demand of $d$ units is applied to all retailers over
a 100-day horizon.  Each node runs a base-stock policy with
$S = d(L+1)$ where $L$ is the lead time from its parent.  The test
confirms that on-hand inventory at each node converges to and stays at
$S - d \cdot L$ (the equilibrium on-hand level) within $L+1$ days.

\subsection{EV2 --- Demand shock response}
\label{sec:meth:ev2}

A synthetic demand series with constant background demand $d$ and a
single large spike at day 20 is read.
The experiment verifies that the upstream nodes observe an amplified order
spike with a delay matching the cumulative pipeline lead time.

\subsection{EV3 --- Policy comparison under transport capacity}
\label{sec:meth:ev3}

Two policies (base-stock and $(s,S)$) are compared on the single-SKU
topology with and without a finite vehicle capacity.  With no capacity
limit both policies track demand similarly.  With capacity limits, the
$(s,S)$ fixed-quantity orders align better with vehicle loads.

\subsection{EV4 --- Capacity scaling}
\label{sec:meth:ev4}

Vehicle capacity is scaled by multipliers $\{0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0\}$
relative to a baseline calibrated to carry one week of average demand.
M5 Walmart demand data is used for realism.  Results are shown in
Table~\ref{tab:ev4} (Section~\ref{sec:results:ev4}).

\section{Dataset: M5 Walmart Forecasting Data}
\label{sec:meth:dataset}

The demand data for all experiments is derived from the
\textbf{M5 Walmart Forecasting dataset}.
The M5 dataset contains daily unit sales for thousands of products across
ten Walmart stores in the United States, spanning approximately five years.

The M5 dataset was selected because it exhibits realistic retail demand
characteristics that stress inventory policies beyond what synthetic
Poisson processes can capture:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Intermittency and spikes.} Sales often cluster around
        promotional events and holidays, creating irregular demand patterns.
  \item \textbf{Weak weekly seasonality.} Weekend peaks and weekday troughs
        introduce low-frequency variation.
  \item \textbf{Long tails.} Occasional large orders produce high
        coefficient-of-variation values that challenge base-stock stability.
\end{itemize}

Per-retailer daily demand CSVs were derived by selecting one M5 series
per retailer per SKU.  For the main experiments, the simulator reads
the \emph{last 365 days} of each series,
which corresponds to the most recent year of Walmart operations in the
M5 timeline.  This choice avoids the non-stationary trend present
across the full 1913-day series while keeping the 365-day horizon long
enough for fill-rate statistics to stabilise.  For the preliminary
validation experiments, a separate synthetic series with a single spike at day 20 was
constructed.

\section{Experimental Controls}
\label{sec:meth:warmup}

At simulation start, all nodes are initialised with a configurable
amount of stock.  This initial stock creates a transient period during
which KPIs reflect the initialisation state rather than the steady-state
behaviour of the policy.  To avoid this bias, an initial window of each
simulation run is \emph{excluded} from all reported KPIs (costs, fill
rates, bullwhip ratios).  Inventory dynamics still execute during the
warm-up window so the system reaches steady state before measurement
begins.

The warm-up length is chosen \emph{per experiment} via the
recommended\_warmup field on each entry of
EXPERIMENT\_META in the runner script.  For the M5 365-day
scenario, this is 30 days — approximately 8\% of the horizon and at
approximately three times the longest pipeline lead time (Supplier$\to$W2 at 6
days plus W2$\to$R3 at 4 days, totalling 10 days).  The CLI --warmup flag
overrides this per run; synthetic configurations whose transient
itself is the signal (e.g.\ the demand-shock validation EV2) pass
--warmup 0 explicitly.  The runner emits a warning if a
non-trivial scenario ($T \geq 200$) is run with no warm-up.

\subsection{Multi-seed statistical validation}
\label{sec:meth:multiseed}

Multi-seed validation is integrated into the experiment runner via the
--validate\_seeds N flag.  When supplied, the runner re-evaluates
the best E3 parameter set in $N$ fresh simulations whose random seeds
are advanced by one each time, and writes
multiseed\_validation.csv (one row per seed) and
multiseed\_summary.csv (mean and standard deviation of total
cost and fill rate).

Because the main experiments use a fixed deterministic M5 demand CSV
\emph{and} deterministic lead times, demand realisations do not vary
across seeds; the multi-seed run therefore reports zero variance under
the canonical configuration.  Cross-seed variance becomes informative
when the demand generator is Poisson or the lead-time generator is
stochastic, which the framework supports but the canonical 1N3 5-SKU
scenario does not exercise.

\section{Bullwhip Metric}
\label{sec:meth:bullwhip}

The bullwhip ratio is computed \emph{per echelon} as the ratio of the
order coefficient-of-variation squared at node $n$ to the
coefficient-of-variation squared of the time series of orders
\emph{arriving at} node $n$:

\begin{equation}
  \text{BW}_{n,s} \;=\;
    \frac{\text{CV}^2\!\bigl(\text{orders out of } n \text{ for SKU } s\bigr)}
         {\text{CV}^2\!\bigl(\text{orders into } n \text{ for SKU } s\bigr)}
  \label{eq:bullwhip}
\end{equation}

The ``orders into $n$'' series is defined as
\begin{itemize}
  \item the external customer demand series, when $n$ is a retailer;
  \item the daily sum of orders\_to\_parent from every child of $n$,
        when $n$ is a warehouse or the supplier.
\end{itemize}

Aggregating child orders is essential for upstream nodes: a warehouse
with two retailers as children faces the \emph{sum} of their orders,
not the demand at any one retailer.  Failing to aggregate produces an
inflated, mis-attributed ratio.  This per-echelon definition follows
\citet{LeeEtAl1997} and is verified by a hand-computable scenario in
tests/test\_bullwhip.py.  Each row of the resulting
bullwhip.csv stores node\_id, sku,
cv2\_in, cv2\_out, and bullwhip\_ratio so the
two component variances can be inspected independently.  The metric is
computed on the post-warm-up window only.

\section{Main Optimisation Experiments E0--E9}
\label{sec:meth:main}

Twelve experiments cover the full study; seven form the core (E0--E4 and
E3\_per\_sku), four (E5--E8) extend the canonical optimum to
disruption, alternative policies, forecast error, and bullwhip-aware
objectives, and one (E9) tests stochastic demand robustness.
Table~\ref{tab:exp-overview} summarises each.

All experiments except E0 use the Optuna-TPE optimiser described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:optimization}.  E4 additionally invokes the
NSGA-II sampler (Section~\ref{sec:opt:pareto}); E6 invokes the
per-policy parameter registry (with joint transport co-optimisation); E8 invokes the bullwhip-aware variant.
E9 does not invoke any optimiser: it applies the E3 parameter set to a Poisson demand
environment and reports transferability.
All experiments share the same network topology to enable fair comparison.

\subsection{Newsvendor levels at runtime (E0)}
\label{sec:meth:newsvendor-runtime}

The analytical baseline E0 uses the newsvendor formula
(Equation~\ref{eq:newsvendor}) with $z_\alpha = 1.64$,
corresponding to a $\sim$95\% one-sided service level.  Crucially, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are
\emph{not} read from the JSON config; they are computed at run time
from the realised demand series after warm-up.  The runner first
performs a dummy simulation to extract the post-warmup demand at every
retailer, aggregates it to obtain warehouse-level demand, derives the
analytical $S$ for each (node, SKU), and overwrites the config's
base\_stock\_level fields and initial\_inventory
before any experiment runs.  The resulting levels are saved to
E0/params.json.  Downstream experiments E1a, E1b, E2, and E3
inherit this corrected baseline so all experiments are anchored at the
same analytical reference.

\subsection{Disruption injection (E5)}
\label{sec:meth:disruption-injection}

Disruptions are passed to the simulator via the disruptions
list of the JSON config.  In E5 a single 14-day outage is injected at
warehouse W1, starting at day $t_s = w + \lfloor 0.6\,(T-w) \rfloor$
where $w$ is the warm-up length and $T$ the horizon, so the shock lands
in the middle of the steady-state measurement window.  During the
outage the node accumulates child orders without dispatching; once the
outage ends, accumulated backlog is served as inventory permits.  The
experiment is run twice: once with the E0 (analytical) parameters and
once with the E3 (joint-optimised) parameters, both unaware of the
disruption at optimisation time.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:meth:reproducibility}

Each experiment run generates a self-contained output directory at
outputs/experiments\_\textlangle ts\textrangle /, containing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item params.json — the exact policy parameters used.
  \item summary.txt — total cost, cost breakdown, fill rates,
        and bullwhip ratios in human-readable form.
  \item CSV logs: inventory\_log.csv, orders\_log.csv,
        costs\_by\_node\_sku.csv, kpis\_by\_sku.csv,
        bullwhip.csv, shipments\_log.csv.
  \item PNG plots: cost breakdown, fill rate, inventory time-series,
        order time-series, bullwhip, and Pareto frontier.
\end{itemize}

The CLI commands to reproduce all results are given in
Appendix~\ref{appn:reproduction}.  The single command that reproduces
the canonical results reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} is

\begin{verbatim}
python scripts/run_experiments.py --config config/1n3_5sku.json \
       --experiments E0 E1a E1b E2 E3 E3_per_sku E4 E5 E6 E7 E8 E9 \
       --trials 100 --validate_seeds 10
python scripts/plot_results.py --expdir outputs/experiments_<ts>/
\end{verbatim}
